# Systems of compensations

**Thesis.** A system that accumulates compensations on its root causes instead of addressing them acquires a property of its own: each layer has side effects that call for the next, each order becomes load-bearing, and the stack feeds itself until the energy put into the system goes to maintaining the stack and the root cannot be reached without collapse.

```
root cause ──▶ symptom ──▶ compensation ──▶ side effects ──▶ compensation ──▶ …
                 ▲                              │               (2nd order)
                 └──────────── new symptoms ◀───┘
   each order anchors; the root is forgotten; energy goes to the stack
```

## The law

A compensation is a layer added beside a problem left in place. Acting on a complex system, its consequences cannot be predicted: side effects emerge, and each is a problem met by another compensation. The orders stack: second, third, each anchored by the ones built on it. Removing one reveals what it held up, so none is removed, and the root layer cannot be addressed without the whole coming down.

The stack feeds itself. The energy is what is put into the system to shape it, the work, attention and money spent on it, and the stack is its resistance: like a cable's, it turns more and more of what goes in into heat, busywork, before it reaches the load. Once the rigidity reaches the system's day-to-day work, the interventions accelerate, side effects synergise, and the system collapses.

The sand pile (Bak, self-organised criticality): the same mass stacked as rocks stands as a tower, poured as sand spreads into a pile capped by its angle of repose, and energy put into sand goes everywhere but up. A compensation stack is sand: the energy spreads into busywork and the system cannot rise above its angle. Prior art, in the weights: Shifting the Burden and Fixes that Fail (Senge, Meadows), policy resistance (Forrester), collapse by diminishing returns on complexity (Tainter), lock-in (Arthur), kludgeocracy (Teles), iatrogenics (Taleb). What this file adds: the recursion by orders, each load-bearing, and the anchoring below.

## Why compensation, not repair

Additivity bias (Adams): the pull is to add a layer, never to remove the cause. A political-cost ratchet holds it in place: repair admits the original model was wrong; a compensation never does. The stack is a ratchet, not a drift.

## Anchoring

A compensation becomes load-bearing by three routes, each a known mechanism, here the step by which a layer locks:

- **Second-order constraint** (Juarrero). The structure that forms around a compensation, roles, teams, contracts, rituals, constrains from above once formed. It now depends on the workflow as such, decoupled from the tension that produced it; fixing the root late no longer dissolves the lock.
- **Sedimentation** (Husserl). The layer is sediment on a flaw whose genesis is forgotten: the unknown known, the expert's curse. Removal requires reactivating what nobody present remembers.
- **Dark constraint** (φconstraint-types). Its effect is seen, its cause is not: it is known by what breaks when it goes.

## Symptoms, signals, causes

A compensation treats a symptom. After a few orders, cause and consequence dissolve into feedback loops: the origin is distributed across the system, any point a plausible cause, and a cause can always be narrated afterward, retrospective coherence (Snowden). The discipline that follows: a signal is read as a symptom pointing to a root still to find. Mistaking the symptom for the cause is what produces the next layer.

## The cliff

The worst case is not degradation but discontinuity: the over-constrained system does not drift back, it falls (Snowden, the clear-to-chaotic boundary), and recovery costs far more than the descent. A managed return stays possible while constraints can still be loosened; the law says where the road ends, not that every system gets there. The fall needs no internal trigger: nothing underneath was resolved, so a radical change in the ecosystem, one that shakes the foundations, brings the whole stack down at once. Good practices applied on top of a stack do not help, and that is expected, not a failure of the practitioners.
